What this does

It compares the price at every sportsbook against a best estimate of the true probability, and flags the cases where a book is paying more than the outcome is worth. It is not predicting who wins — it is finding prices that disagree with the rest of the market.

How a pick is made

  1. Pull odds from ~23 books, every few minutes, faster near kickoff.
  2. Remove the vig. Posted odds add up to more than 100% — that gap is the book's margin. We strip it per book to get a fair probability.
  3. Anchor on the sharp book. Pinnacle takes big bets and doesn't limit winners, so its price is the closest thing to truth. Where it's available, it sets fair value rather than an average that includes the book we'd be betting into.
  4. Shop every book for the best available number on that same bet.
  5. Edge = what we'd be paid, minus what it's worth.
  6. Rank it by how far the price sits from fair. How much to bet is your call, not ours.

Tiers

Four gates. A pick must clear all of them.

Book count proves the market is real, not one stale price. Dispersion is how much the books disagree — a big edge against a scattered market usually means we're the ones who are wrong.

Why CLV matters more than wins

Over a few hundred bets, win rate is mostly luck — you can be genuinely good and still finish down. Closing line value measures whether you got a better number than the market settled on. It's the thing that shows up first, and profit follows it. Judge the system on CLV.

What it doesn't do

  • No AI is guessing at scores. Language models are bad at numbers; the probabilities come from statistics, not chat.
  • It won't find edges every day. Main spreads and totals are among the most efficiently priced markets in the world — an empty list is usually the honest answer.
  • It can't see everything. A coach announcing a snap count on Friday isn't in any dataset.
